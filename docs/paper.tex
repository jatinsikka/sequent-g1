\begin{abstract}
We present Sequent, an end-to-end system that takes a plant-floor incident ticket written in an operator's own words, retrieves the correct Standard Operating Procedure (SOP) from a library of 1{,}012 procedures, converts it into a faithful step-by-step plan, and executes that plan on a simulated Unitree G1 humanoid (walking, picking, carrying, placing, pressing a button, and partially pulling a lever) in one continuous MuJoCo simulation, with every step verified against measured physics rather than the policy's own claim. The ``brain'' is a fine-tuned 22.7M-parameter sentence bi-encoder that reaches Recall@1 $0.577 \pm 0.006$ / Recall@5 0.856 / MRR 0.695 (5 seeds) on a held-out test set where TF-IDF scores 0.301, BM25 0.357 and the same encoder without fine-tuning 0.409; the gain over the untuned encoder holds on held-out SOPs (0.656 vs.\ 0.468) and held-out equipment families (0.607 vs.\ 0.420), and an off-the-shelf cross-encoder reranker \emph{lowers} R@1 (0.452). The ``body'' follows an explicit architecture thesis: classical controllers for kinematics (walking, IK-based picking), reinforcement learning for contact-rich interaction (button press, lever pull), and a physics verifier above both. This division was chosen based on the results in Section 4.2: RL grasping on the gripper-equipped humanoid scored 0\% verified success across the reward configurations we tried, the ``lifts'' recorded by the training metric were the box popping ballistically out of a crushing pinch, and grasping was replaced by an IK controller with a force-gated latch. The paper documents the RL manipulation work in detail: a curriculum plus behavior-cloning warm-starts (used twice, with measured limits), an action envelope physically too small to express the task, a catalog of reward hacks (parking, seat farming, pumping, air-swinging, penalty-hacked smoothness, metric-fooled lifts), each caught by human review of rendered rollouts rather than by metrics and each fixed at the root, and a description of the erratic behavior the final policies still exhibit. The final demonstration (a pipeline demonstration: its ticket and SOP were authored together) retrieves the SOP at rank 1 (score 0.897) and executes a five-action plan in one take, with the system reporting the lever step as failed and the ticket as ``partially resolved.'' All numbers reported here were measured by us, and failed steps are reported as failed.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Robot-learning pipelines can fail silently: a policy reports success because its own reward said so, and a demo reel shows the takes that worked. Sequent is built around the opposite discipline: the robot executes written procedures, and a verification layer measures, against simulator state rather than the policy's claim, whether each step actually happened.

The full loop is:
\begin{quote}
\textbf{operator ticket} $\to$ \textbf{retrieval} over 1{,}012 SOPs $\to$ \textbf{faithful plan} (one skill per written SOP step) $\to$ \textbf{execution} on a simulated Unitree G1 (MuJoCo, with the AMO whole-body controller \cite{amo} driving the legs and a Robotiq 2F-85 gripper grafted onto the right wrist) $\to$ \textbf{per-step physics verification} $\to$ \textbf{final verdict}.
\end{quote}

The executed skills are: \texttt{walk\_to} (controller), \texttt{pick}/\texttt{place} (controller: damped-least-squares IK plus a force-gated latch), \texttt{press\_button} (reinforcement learning), \texttt{lever} (reinforcement learning, partially reliable), and \texttt{notify}. The final demonstration runs walk $\to$ pick $\to$ carry $\to$ place $\to$ press $\to$ lever $\to$ notify in \emph{one continuous simulation} with on-screen verification of every step, and reports its own lever failure on camera.

This paper makes three kinds of contribution:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{A working system} connecting language-level intent (a messy human ticket) to physical task execution through retrieval-grounded planning, with verification as the connective tissue.
  \item \textbf{Measured retrieval results on a deliberately hard synthetic SOP benchmark}, reproduced over 5 seeds against lexical (TF-IDF, BM25) and untuned neural baselines, with held-out-SOP and held-out-family generalization tests, a breakdown by how clearly the operator writes, and one negative result (a generic reranker that degraded a strong retriever).
  \item \textbf{A detailed post-mortem of RL manipulation}, documenting why RL grasping failed outright and became a controller (\S\ref{sec:graspfail}), how behavior cloning bridged the exploration gap twice and where it capped out (\S\ref{sec:bc}), what residual erratic behavior the final policies still exhibit (\S\ref{sec:erratic}), and a full catalog of reward hacks with their fixes (\S\ref{sec:hacks}).
\end{enumerate}

A note on methodology: every number in this paper was measured by us on our own runs. Where a component is unreliable (the lever), we say so and quantify it. Where a demo element was authored for the demo (the finale's SOP), we disclose it.

The \emph{brain} (corpus generation, retriever training and evaluation; \texttt{brain/benchmark/}) and the \emph{robot} (the MuJoCo G1 model, skill controllers and RL environments, the verifier, and the demo harnesses) live in one repository, \texttt{sequent-g1}. At runtime the brain is bridged into the robot process (\texttt{brain\_bridge.py}), so a single script takes a ticket string and drives the simulated robot.

\paragraph{Execution model.} The plan is a typed list of skills with arguments. An executor runs each skill inside a contract: a \emph{precondition} check (measured from \texttt{mjData}; e.g., robot upright, base within tolerance of the workstation), the skill itself, and a \emph{postcondition} check (e.g., button displaced past threshold and held; box held above the table for a sustained window; base still upright). A step that fails its postcondition is reported as failed rather than silently passing, and the final verdict aggregates step results.

\paragraph{Embodiment.} One robot for everything: the 29-DoF Unitree G1 with the frozen AMO whole-body controller (RSS 2025) balancing and locomoting the legs and torso at 50\,Hz, and a Robotiq 2F-85 parallel gripper attached to the right wrist. Earlier iterations used separate rigs for manipulation and locomotion; unifying them (rebuilding collision masks and re-addressing the controller's DoF reads around the appended wrist and gripper joints) was a prerequisite for any continuous demo.

\section{The Brain: Retrieval-Grounded Planning}

\subsection{A deliberately hard corpus}

The project's original 100-SOP corpus was lexically trivial: TF-IDF keyword search already retrieved at ${\approx}0.96$ R@1, so a neural retriever had nothing to prove. We rebuilt the benchmark to be hard in the way real plants are hard:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{1{,}012 SOPs} modeling one automotive-components plant across \textbf{46 equipment families} (hydraulic press, CNC mill, conveyor, boiler, electrical panel, \ldots), ${\sim}22$ SOPs per family.
  \item \textbf{Within-family hard negatives}: procedures that differ only in meaning: pressure \emph{below setpoint} vs.\ \emph{sudden collapse} vs.\ \emph{above ceiling}; warning vs.\ emergency.
  \item \textbf{2{,}338 incidents written in operator voice}: colloquial, symptom-first, deliberately avoiding the SOP's own title words, so retrieval cannot win by keyword overlap. Split \textbf{1{,}859 / 210 / 269} into train / validation / held-out test.
\end{itemize}
Every SOP is executable by the robot's 7-skill vocabulary (\texttt{walk\_to, press\_button, wait, read\_sensor, pick, place, notify}).

\subsection{The retriever: a fine-tuned MiniLM bi-encoder}

We fine-tuned \texttt{sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2} (a 22.7M-parameter, 6-layer, 384-dimensional bi-encoder) with MultipleNegativesRankingLoss \cite{sbert,minilm} for 3 epochs (batch 32) on the 1{,}859 training pairs, in about 2 minutes on a laptop. Each incident is pulled toward its gold SOP and pushed away from the other SOPs in its batch. Evaluation ranks the full 1{,}012-SOP index for each of the 269 held-out incidents (Table~\ref{tab:retrieval-main}); fine-tuned rows are mean $\pm$ std over 5 seeds.

\paragraph{Generalization.} Every test SOP also has training incidents, so we additionally hold out (i) 20\% of SOPs entirely and (ii) five whole equipment families. The fine-tuned model reaches R@1 0.656 and 0.607 against 0.468 and 0.420 untuned (each split has its own test set, so only within-split comparisons are meaningful): it learns how operators describe problems, not just the training SOPs.

\paragraph{How clearly the operator writes matters most.} We compare the vague test incidents, the same incidents with the machine named, and 92 clear tickets written by an operator who knows the system (two per family, sampled with a fixed seed and written by the author from each SOP's title and condition, so they share some of its vocabulary). R@1 for BM25 / fine-tuned (3 seeds): 0.357 / 0.577, 0.398 / 0.632, and 0.837 / 0.920 (Figure~\ref{fig:retrieval}). Clear tickets are easy for every method.

\paragraph{Where it fails.} About 70\% of misses pick an SOP for a different machine. Naming the machine raises the share of top-1 picks on the correct machine from 0.71 to 0.85 but R@1 only to 0.63, so the remaining errors are same-machine look-alikes. The model always returns its nearest SOP and cannot say ``none of these'' (\S\ref{sec:future}).

\paragraph{An off-the-shelf reranker hurts.} Reranking the top-10 with \texttt{ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2} lowers R@1 from 0.577 to 0.452; it is excluded from the system.

\subsection{The planner: deterministic and faithful, by evidence}

The planner is \textbf{deterministic}: one skill per written SOP step, order preserved, with implicit \texttt{walk\_to} navigation inserted when the required location changes. This works because the corpus SOPs are written at skill granularity (\texttt{press\_button estop\_button}); step arguments are mapped onto the simulator's object set (four colored buttons, a fixed tool set). The learned component of the brain is the retriever; the planner involves no learning. An earlier learned planner (Flan-T5-base with LoRA, emitting JSON) is not reported as a result: its setup could not succeed, since T5's tokenizer has no \texttt{\{} or \texttt{\}} and it was trained on the earlier 100-SOP corpus with keyword-template targets. A correctly set-up learned planner, needed for SOPs written in ordinary prose, is future work.

\subsection{Runtime retrieval}

At runtime (\texttt{brain\_bridge.py}) the fine-tuned model's ranking is interleaved with a lexical TF-IDF ranking, semantic first, so the top-1 pick is the semantic model's; lexical candidates fill later slots. With an API key, \texttt{run\_task.py --planner llm} passes the top-5 candidates to an LLM that picks one. Neither the interleaving nor the LLM picker is evaluated here; the numbers above are for the fine-tuned model alone. The finale script queries the fine-tuned model directly.

\section{The Body: Controllers for Kinematics, RL for Contact}

\subsection{Architecture thesis}

The body's design follows one thesis, arrived at from the results below:
\begin{quote}
\textbf{Controllers for kinematics; reinforcement learning for contact-rich interaction; verification above both.}
\end{quote}
Where the task is a geometric problem with a known answer (walk to a pose, servo a gripper to a grasp point), a controller is more reliable, more debuggable, and has predictable failure modes. Where the task is dominated by contact dynamics that are difficult to model (how hard to push a spring-loaded button, how to drive a latched lever through its arc), RL is the appropriate tool. The verifier does not care which produced the motion; it measures outcomes.

We did not start with this thesis; we adopted it based on the results in the next subsection.

\subsection{Why RL grasping failed and why the pick is a controller}
\label{sec:graspfail}

This deserves its own subsection because it is the single most instructive negative result in the project, and because we initially misdiagnosed it twice (first as a reward problem, then as a sample-budget problem).

\paragraph{What happened.} We trained RL grasp-and-lift across roughly \textbf{ten reward/configuration variants}: lift saturation, grip-continuity gating, hold-income shaping, anti-yank velocity penalties, mass/friction randomization, curriculum spawns. \textbf{Deterministic verified success was 0 across all of them.} Worse, the training metrics repeatedly \emph{looked} like progress: logged ``lift'' rates of 30--60\%. Watching the rendered rollouts showed what the metric could not: the box was being squeezed out of a \textbf{crushing pinch and popping upward ballistically}. The height check fired while the object was airborne and ungripped. The policy had learned to launch the box, not to lift it.

\paragraph{Why it happened: three compounding causes.}
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{A contact-fidelity wall.} Stable parallel-jaw force closure lives in a narrow regime of the simulator's contact solver: two opposing pad contacts with balanced normal forces, maintained across timesteps. The solver resolves a hard pinch on a box as an unstable extrusion problem: squeeze slightly too hard or slightly off-center and the box shoots out. The physics the policy must exploit is precisely the physics the simulator represents least faithfully.
  \item \textbf{A dithering grip never achieves force closure.} An RL policy's actions are exploration-noised and re-decided every step; the resulting grip force oscillates. Force closure needs a \emph{held} constraint, and a stochastic policy is structurally bad at holding one; our contact traces showed the two-sided grip flag flickering on and off as the box tumbled between the pads.
  \item \textbf{The reward could not tell held from launched.} Any instantaneous height- or displacement-based lift signal is satisfied by a ballistic pop. Only a post-hoc \emph{held-state} check (grip maintained AND object supported for a sustained window) distinguishes them, and that check is a verifier, not a reward one can descend.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{The fix was to stop using RL.} The pick became a controller: damped-least-squares IK plus a force-gated latch (\S\ref{sec:pick}), which held the lift on 11 of 12 validation attempts on the first day it existed. The lesson: \textbf{parallel-jaw grasping is a controller problem (industry treats it as one), and RL added risk with no benefit here.} More samples could not fix it; we considered and rejected a GPU-parallel port for this reason, since more samples from the same flawed contact regime would not have changed the outcome. RL was redirected to the tasks it suits: contact-rich button pressing and lever pulling, where the required behavior is a \emph{motion through contact} rather than a \emph{held constraint}.

\subsection{Walking: AMO plus a yaw servo}

Locomotion uses the frozen AMO whole-body controller with velocity commands from a simple navigation layer. AMO as wired has limitations, which we measured: it \textbf{cannot turn in place}, it carries a \textbf{${\sim}18$\degs{} yaw bias while walking} (it crabs), and it \textbf{drifts under payload} (the carried box shifts the CoM it was not trained for). The navigation layer wraps it in a \textbf{closed-loop yaw servo} (continuously re-steering toward the target from measured base pose) plus arc-turn maneuvers for large heading changes. This works in practice: the finale's carry to the panel station includes a 180\degs{} turn with the box held.

\subsection{Picking: IK plus a force-gated latch}
\label{sec:pick}

The pick is a controller. Damped-least-squares IK servos the gripper above and onto the object; the gripper closes; a \textbf{force-gated latch} engages only when measured pad contact forces confirm a real two-sided grip, after which the object is constrained to the hand for the carry. The latch cannot engage across a gap, so a ``grasp'' is by construction a physical pinch. The validated pick achieves an \textbf{11.9\,cm held lift} in the continuous walk$\to$pick demo (11/12 held lifts in the standalone validation), with the block staying on the tabletop until lifted (no yanking, no teleporting).

\subsection{Verified execution}

Every step in every demo runs inside the pre/postcondition contract of \S2. The verifier predates most of the skills and proved useful early: pointed at our best pre-rebuild grasp checkpoints, it showed one policy's claimed 30\% success was a \textbf{verified 0\%} (it grasped and never lifted), while a sibling checkpoint's claimed 60\% was a verified 55\%, a real skill. Distinguishing those two cases is the core function of the verifier.

\section{RL Skills: Reward Design and Its Failure Modes}

This section is the paper's core. Each subsection follows the same shape: \emph{design $\to$ observed exploit or failure $\to$ fix $\to$ why the fix is the right one.} All behaviors described were observed in rendered rollouts; all numbers are measured.

\subsection{The reach curriculum: from contact dead-zone to learned reach}
\label{sec:curriculum}

\paragraph{Design.} Train \texttt{press\_button} end-to-end with PPO: the arm starts at rest, the policy must reach ${\sim}19$\,cm to the button and press.

\paragraph{Failure 1: the contact dead-zone.} From rest, random exploration essentially never touches the button, so a contact-gated reward provides zero gradient. Training from rest yielded nothing.

\paragraph{Failure 2: seeded starts learn only the push.} Seeding the episode \emph{in contact} (IK places the gripper on the button at reset) trains reliably, but then RL has only learned the final push; the reach, the harder part, comes from the IK seed rather than from learning.

\paragraph{Fix: a two-point curriculum.} At reset the arm is interpolated between the contact pose (fraction 0) and the true rest pose (fraction 1); a success-gated callback advances the fraction whenever the recent success rate clears a threshold, so the learned reach grows from millimeters to the full envelope. Two further fixes were required to make it converge:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Target the cap face, not the body origin.} The reach reward initially measured distance to the button \emph{body} origin, which sits 7.7\,cm behind the pressable cap face. That hidden offset flattened the reward gradient exactly where precision mattered; re-targeting the cap face restored it.
  \item \textbf{Anti-parking.} The policy learned to hover ${\sim}5$\,cm short of the button, farming a wide proximity band's income forever. We narrowed the proximity band from 0.20\,m to 0.05\,m (no far plateau to camp on) and doubled the distance-penalty pull, so a parked hand earns strictly less than a committed press.
\end{itemize}
After both fixes the deterministic policy reaches from the true rest pose and presses 28--31\,mm, at every curriculum level, with no IK seed.

\subsection{The action-envelope wall}
\label{sec:envelope}

\paragraph{Failure.} A full day of far-start training failures (across reward variants, entropy schedules, and curriculum settings) traced to one number. The policy's action scale was \textbf{0.5\,rad} per joint around the neutral pose, but the rest$\to$contact joint-space offsets are \textbf{0.511\,rad (shoulder yaw)} and \textbf{0.672\,rad (elbow)} for the press, and up to \textbf{1.1\,rad} for the lever. From a true rest start the policy \emph{physically could not command the contact pose}; every attempt saturated silently at the actuator clamp, and no amount of learning could fix it.

\paragraph{Fix.} Widen the envelope: 0.8\,rad for the press, 1.2\,rad for the lever. Far-start learning unblocked immediately.

\centering

\paragraph{Lesson.} Before debugging rewards, verify that the action space can \emph{express} the optimal policy. Saturation is silent; a clamped action looks identical to a timid one in every training curve.

\subsection{Smoothness: low-pass filtering instead of penalties}
\label{sec:smooth}

\paragraph{Design.} Penalize jerk in the reward to get smooth arm motion.

\paragraph{Failure.} The penalty was reward-hacked: the policy learned to flail the arm rapidly, and the fast oscillation bumped the button often enough that the press income outweighed the jerk penalty. Measured jerk: \textbf{0.83} (rad/step$^2$ proxy). The metric said ``pressing''; the video showed thrashing.

\paragraph{Fix.} Move smoothness from the reward to the \textbf{control level}: a low-pass filter on the arm command ($\alpha = 0.12$) makes high-frequency flailing physically impossible. Measured jerk dropped to \textbf{0.001} with the press intact.

\paragraph{Lesson.} When a constraint is hackable as a penalty, make it structural. A reward penalty can be traded off against other income; a control-level filter cannot.

\subsection{Press-once: closing the income loops}
\label{sec:pressonce}

\paragraph{Failure 1: pumping.} The per-step depth income made \emph{re-pressing} profitable: the deterministic policy pressed, released, and pressed again, farming the depth ramp each cycle. Caught by the operator watching the demo, not by any metric.

\paragraph{Fix 1.} Success terminates the episode with a bonus: one press, held, then the episode ends, leaving nothing to farm.

\paragraph{Failure 2: air-swinging.} With the press terminated on success, the policy discovered that \emph{motion after the press was free} and swung the arm through the air while the hold counter ran.

\paragraph{Fix 2.} A post-press stillness cost, plus a \textbf{dip-tolerant hold counter} (brief sub-threshold dips do not reset the hold, so the policy is not punished into micro-pumping to keep the counter alive).

\paragraph{Result.} PUMPS=0, measured live inside the demo. The deterministic policy is \textbf{8/8 across near and far starts} (29--40\,mm press depth, held).

\paragraph{Stance robustness.} The walk does not deliver the robot to a perfect stance. We first trained with $\pm 4$\,cm / $\pm 5$\degs{} arrival noise, then \emph{measured} the loaded walk's real arrival error (about 6\,cm / 10\degs) and widened the training noise to $\pm 8$\,cm / $\pm 12$\degs. Train for the distribution you will actually be handed, and measure that distribution rather than assuming it.

\subsection{The lever: the hardest skill (12+ iterations, partially solved)}
\label{sec:lever}

The lever is where every failure mode above recurred at once, plus three of its own.

\paragraph{Mechanism redesign.} The original spring-return lever erased every partial attempt: any exploratory nudge sprang back, so no progress accumulated across an episode. We redesigned it as a \textbf{breaker-style latching pull-down}: resting latched \emph{up} at 1.05\,rad, target 0.15\,rad, with a friction latch and no return spring. The latch turns exploration into a \textbf{ratchet}: partial progress persists, so the policy can discover the arc incrementally.

\paragraph{Two-axis curriculum.} A single curriculum fraction drives \emph{both} the arm's start distance and the lever's start angle. The full 0.63\,rad arc was undiscoverable from a cold start; letting early training begin with the lever partially thrown makes completion reachable while the reach grows.

\paragraph{Exploit: seat farming.} The lever's seat gives ${\sim}0.17$\,rad of free travel. Linear progress income paid an annuity for sitting in the seat; the policy parked there. Fix: re-baseline income to the seat (and make income convex in progress), so real reward exists only near completion.

\paragraph{Quiet exploration.} SB3's default Gaussian exploration noise was violent enough to fling the latched lever on nearly every rollout, so the training data itself indicated that the lever ``just moves,'' poisoning the value function. Setting the policy's initial $\log\sigma$ to $\mathbf{-1.5}$ (quiet exploration) produced the largest single improvement: the curriculum, stuck at 0, advanced $0 \to 0.9$ immediately after the change.

\paragraph{BC warm-start.} As with the original press, the recipe that finally produced full throws was \textbf{behavior cloning from scripted IK arc-follow demonstrations}, a \textbf{pull-only DAgger} pass \cite{dagger} (corrective labels only on the pull phase), then PPO fine-tuning with an entropy crush to consolidate the deterministic behavior.

\paragraph{Result and the identified structural cause.} First full deterministic throw: \textbf{51\degs{} into the success band}, but only ${\sim}50\%$ reliable across starts, with jerky motion. The root cause is identified and structural, not tuning: the environment's per-episode \texttt{arm\_reach\_bias} (a stance-dependent offset applied to the arm neutral) is \textbf{not in the policy's observation}. Identical observed states therefore demand \emph{different} actions in different episodes, an action-semantics ambiguity that no amount of training can resolve. The fix is designed (add the bias to the observation, $33 \to 37$ dims; re-collect demos; re-BC; retrain) but not yet executed; we report the lever as a partial result.

\subsection{Behavior cloning as the exploration bridge: used twice, with known limits}
\label{sec:bc}

Both contact skills that ultimately worked were unlocked by the same recipe, and it deserves explicit treatment because pure PPO failed both times first.

\paragraph{Where we used it.}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{The original button press (bp-v5).} Pure PPO stalled through three reward iterations (deterministic hover at 0.04--0.96\,cm of press depth while the stochastic policy pressed 8/10; the mean of the policy had never experienced press income). The fix was a \textbf{scripted closed-loop IK demonstration $\to$ behavior cloning $\to$ PPO fine-tune}: the demo puts the mean policy \emph{at} the press, BC copies it, PPO then optimizes from a basin where the press reward actually has gradient.
  \item \textbf{The lever.} Same recipe, extended: \textbf{scripted IK arc-follow demonstrations + a pull-only DAgger pass $\to$ BC $\to$ PPO with entropy crush.} This produced the first full deterministic throw after 12 pure-RL iterations had produced none.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Why it works.} BC does not teach the task; it teaches the \emph{neighborhood}. A contact-gated reward is a cliff: zero everywhere the policy actually visits, informative only in a region random exploration essentially never reaches (\S\ref{sec:curriculum}). BC places the policy inside the region where reward gradients exist, converting an undiscoverable exploration problem into an ordinary optimization one. The curriculum (\S\ref{sec:curriculum}) and BC are two solutions to the same problem; we used the curriculum where a natural interpolation existed (arm start pose) and BC where the required motion was a coordinated arc no interpolation would find.

\paragraph{The limits we hit.} BC caps at its teacher: on far starts the cloned lever policy succeeded only ${\sim}35\%$, roughly the scripted teacher's own reliability there. And DAgger \emph{regressed} when we extended it to reach states: because of the action-semantics ambiguity of \S\ref{sec:lever}, the expert's corrective labels for identical observations differed across episodes, so aggregation taught the policy the average of two incompatible answers. BC inherits every observability flaw of the environment it is cloned in; it is a bridge into the reward basin, not a substitute for a well-posed MDP.

\subsection{Residual erratic behavior: what the final policies still look like}
\label{sec:erratic}

We report this because polished demo GIFs can imply a smoothness the policies do not uniformly have.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{The press}, though 8/8 deterministic and PUMPS=0, still shows \textbf{visible residual motion artifacts on some episodes}: small oscillatory corrections around contact and occasional hesitation on approach.
  \item \textbf{The lever motion is jerky and laggy-looking}: rapid corrections, stalls mid-arc, and spring-backs against the latch. Quantitatively: the deterministic best across a 4-variant stance sweep drove the lever to \textbf{0.443\,rad} (the other variants reached 0.595 and 0.627), against a success bar of $\mathbf{< 0.25}$\,rad; the best stance variant got 69\% of the way through the arc and stalled, and overall reliability is ${\sim}50\%$.
\end{itemize}

The causes are the ones this section has cataloged:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Exploration-shaped policies.} Policies trained under Gaussian action noise learn corrections \emph{for} that noise; deterministic playback retains the twitchy texture even with the noise off. The entropy crush reduces but does not eliminate it.
  \item \textbf{The low-pass filter trades flail for lag.} The $\alpha = 0.12$ command filter that killed the jerk hack (\S\ref{sec:smooth}) also makes the arm respond sluggishly to legitimate corrections: smooth, but visibly laggy near contact.
  \item \textbf{Stance sensitivity.} The walk's arrival distribution ($\pm 6$\,cm / 10\degs{} measured) moves the lever arc relative to the arm workspace; the 0.443/0.595/0.627 spread across stance variants is this sensitivity made visible.
  \item \textbf{The \texttt{arm\_reach\_bias} observability flaw (\S\ref{sec:lever}).} The policy cannot condition on the one variable that changes what its actions mean, so it hedges, and hedging appears as dithering.
\end{enumerate}
None of these is mysterious; all are consequences of documented design choices, and the first and fourth have designed fixes. The final state of RL manipulation is: \emph{reliable and verified on the press; about half-reliable, with visibly rough motion, on the lever.}

\subsection{PPO and infrastructure details}

All RL skills use Stable-Baselines3 PPO \cite{ppo,sb3}, MlpPolicy $[128, 128]$, \texttt{n\_steps} 1024, batch 1024, $\gamma = 0.99$, entropy coefficient 0.02 for the press and 0.05 for the lever, followed for the lever by a final low-entropy phase. Training runs on 24--56 parallel MuJoCo \cite{mujoco} environments on 32- and 64-core Azure VMs. One infrastructure bug mattered enough to report: with default threading, 32 AMO workers each spawned a full complement of torch threads and thrashed the cores, giving 18 env steps/s. Capping \texttt{OMP\_NUM\_THREADS=1} per worker (with the learner's threads uncapped separately) took throughput to ${\sim}2{,}600$ steps/s, a $144\times$ recovery that turned overnight runs into 15-minute iterations.

\section{Methodology: Human Review of Rendered Rollouts}

One practice recurs through the results above:

\textbf{Every reward hack in this project was found by a human watching rendered rollouts; none was found by a metric.} The full catalog is in \S\ref{sec:hacks}: the grasp ``lifts'' were a box popping out of the grip and passing the height check mid-air; the ``presses'' were a policy cycling the button to re-earn depth reward; the seat-farming lever sat in free travel collecting progress reward. In each case the metric reported success while the video showed the exploit. The operating rules that follow:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Frame-check before claiming.} No run is logged as a success until its deterministic rollout has been watched.
  \item \textbf{Verify semantics, not scalars.} The verifier's postconditions encode what the task \emph{means} (held, sustained, upright), not what is easy to measure (peak height, threshold crossing).
  \item \textbf{Only measured numbers.} Nothing appears on the website, in the logs, or in this paper that we did not measure ourselves. This rule led to two retractions: a previously reported planner result that turned out to be a hardcoded placeholder, and an early grasp result trained on a broken simulation.
  \item \textbf{Root-cause before retrain.} The action-envelope hunt (\S\ref{sec:envelope}) cost a day but ended a class of failures; retraining on top of an unexpressible action space would have burned the same day with nothing to show.
\end{enumerate}

We believe this section generalizes beyond this project: reward hacking is usually presented as an alignment curiosity, but in practice it is a \emph{routine engineering hazard} whose only reliable detector, at this scale, is human review of behavior.

\section{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Simulation only.} Everything runs in MuJoCo. No sim-to-real transfer is claimed or attempted; contact parameters were tuned for plausibility, not matched to hardware.
  \item \textbf{Synthetic SOP corpus, same-generator test.} The retrieval test set is held out from training but produced by the same generator as the corpus. There is no out-of-distribution or real-plant evaluation; real-world retrieval numbers would differ.
  \item \textbf{The lever is about 50\% reliable (1 of 2 deterministic episodes) and jerky.} The structural cause (unobserved per-episode reach bias) is identified and the fix designed, but not yet executed. We report it as a partial skill.
  \item \textbf{Handoff seams re-initialize controller state.} Transitions between skills restore controller state at the seam (the base is pinned, nothing teleports, and scene objects persist), but a reset does exist at each handoff; the demo is continuous in physics, not in controller memory.
  \item \textbf{Single operator-verifier.} The human-review methodology has $n{=}1$ reviewer; the same eyes that designed the rewards audited them.
  \item \textbf{The finale SOP was authored for the demo.} SOP-1013 was written by us and inserted into the index; retrieval over the 1{,}012 pre-existing SOPs is trained and evaluated, but the finale's specific retrieval target is not an organic corpus member. Disclosed here and in the demo.
\end{itemize}

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Lever rebuild with the observation fix}: add \texttt{arm\_reach\_bias} to the policy observation, re-collect demonstrations, re-run BC $\to$ DAgger $\to$ PPO. This is designed and queued; we expect it to close the action-semantics ambiguity that caps reliability at ${\sim}50\%$.
  \item \textbf{GPU-parallel simulation (Isaac Lab).} The curriculum results show that CPU MuJoCo can train these skills; a port would buy iteration speed for the harder contact tasks and should be judged on that basis. Our earlier plan to add throughput when the blocker was contact fidelity (\S\ref{sec:graspfail}) is the mistake to avoid repeating.
  \item \textbf{Hardware.} The architecture (controllers + RL + verifier) was chosen with a real G1 in mind; the verifier's contracts translate naturally to estimated state. Sim-to-real for the RL contact skills is the open risk.
  \item \textbf{Retrieval beyond the generator.} An out-of-distribution test set, ideally real maintenance tickets, is the necessary next measurement before any claim about deployed retrieval quality.
  \item \textbf{Ask when unsure.} The retriever always acts on its nearest SOP. Using the top-1 score (low for out-of-scope tickets) and the top-1/top-2 margin (near zero for ambiguous ones such as ``the machine is overheating'') to refuse, escalate, or ask ``which machine?'' is the most important safety addition.
  \item \textbf{Use the machine ID.} Real tickets come from a known machine; filtering candidates to that equipment family targets the 70\% of errors that pick the wrong machine.
  \item \textbf{Hard-negative training} against same-machine look-alike SOPs instead of random in-batch negatives.
  \item \textbf{An LLM baseline and a learned planner}: measure an LLM that picks among the retriever's candidates, and train a planner (with an output format its tokenizer can express) for SOPs written in ordinary prose.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

Sequent connects a human's plain-language description of a problem, through retrieval of the governing procedure, to a humanoid physically executing that procedure, with a verifier above the whole stack that reports ``partially resolved'' when a step fails. The headline artifacts are a fine-tuned retriever that improves on lexical and untuned neural baselines and generalizes to unseen procedures and machines, and a one-take loco-manipulation chain; the durable contributions, in our view, are the negative results and the methodology. Controllers outperformed RL where the task was kinematic (parallel-jaw grasping most of all), and RL was the right tool where contact dominated. Control-level constraints outperformed reward penalties. In every case where a policy found a way to collect reward without doing the task, the exploit was caught by a human watching rendered rollouts, not by a metric.
