\begin{abstract}
Factories run on written Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs), but
operators report problems in their own words. We built Sequent, a system
in which a simulated Unitree G1 humanoid reads such a report, retrieves
the SOP that applies out of 1,012, maps its steps to robot skills, and
executes them in one continuous MuJoCo simulation. A verifier checks
every step against the simulator state instead of trusting the policy,
so a step that fails is reported as failed. The retriever is a small
transformer fine-tuned with a contrastive loss. On vague operator
tickets it ranks the correct SOP first 57.7\% of the time (5 seeds),
against 35.7\% for BM25 and 40.9\% for the same model without
fine-tuning, and it keeps most of this gain on SOPs and machine families
held out from training. For the body we use controllers where the
problem is geometric (walking, grasping) and reinforcement learning
where contact makes modeling hard (pressing a button, pulling a lever).
Neither RL contact skill is solved. The button-press policy learned to
farm reward by pressing repeatedly; we stopped it by ending the episode
after the first held press rather than fixing the reward. The lever
succeeds about half the time. We also report six reward exploits found
by inspecting rendered rollouts.
\end{abstract}

Industrial plants document their responses to faults as written Standard
Operating Procedures (SOPs). Operators report faults in their own words,
for example ``the dispenser coasted to a stop and the thermal light is
red,'' without naming the procedure. A robot that maps such a report to
an approved SOP and executes it would be useful without open-ended
planning.

Recent language-driven robot systems use large language models to turn
instructions into plans {[}1, 2, 3{]}. A freely generated plan, however,
is not tied to an approved procedure, and success is often judged by the
model's own report or by the policy's reward. In a plant, the plan
should come from an approved SOP, and a step should count as done only
if the physical state changed.

Our approach makes three choices. First, we retrieve rather than
generate: a retriever trained on operator tickets selects an existing
SOP, so the robot runs only approved procedures. Second, we verify from
simulator state: each skill runs inside a precondition and postcondition
check that does not use the policy's output. Third, we use controllers
for the geometric skills (walking, grasping) and RL for the
contact-dominated ones (pressing, pulling).

\textbf{Related work.} Our work relates to four lines of research.
\emph{Language-conditioned task planning} uses LLMs to produce skill
sequences, scored by affordances {[}1{]}, written as code {[}2{]}, or
revised with feedback {[}3{]}. These plan freely; we restrict the robot
to retrieved, pre-approved procedures, which trades flexibility for
auditability. \emph{Dense retrieval} trains bi-encoders on
question--passage pairs {[}4, 5{]} and underlies retrieval-augmented
generation {[}6{]}; lexical methods such as BM25 {[}7{]} remain strong
baselines. We apply a standard bi-encoder recipe to a new setting,
maintenance tickets against SOPs with many look-alike procedures, and
measure where it fails. \emph{Knowing when to ask} has been studied for
LLM planners using calibrated uncertainty {[}8{]}; our retriever does
not yet abstain, and we measure what a simple confidence cutoff would
give. \emph{Humanoid loco-manipulation and reward design}: we build on
the AMO whole-body controller {[}9{]} for walking, and contrast with
systems that learn manipulation from demonstrations {[}10{]}. The reward
hacks we document are instances of specification gaming {[}11, 12{]}.

\textbf{Contributions.} In light of prior work, our contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  A system that goes from an operator's ticket to a verified,
  multi-skill humanoid execution in one continuous simulation: walk,
  pick, carry, place, press, and a partial lever pull, with every step
  checked against simulator state.
\item
  A retrieval benchmark of 1,012 SOPs across 46 equipment families with
  2,338 operator-style incidents, and a reproducible evaluation: the
  fine-tuned retriever reaches R@1 0.577 ± 0.006 versus 0.357 for BM25
  and 0.409 untuned, with held-out-SOP, held-out-family, and
  ticket-clarity tests. Code, data, and per-run results are released.
\item

  gripper-equipped humanoid reached 0\% verified success, while an IK

\item
  Six reward exploits observed during training, identified from rendered
  rollouts, and the fix applied to each (pumping was stopped by early
  termination, not fixed).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Problem Statement}\label{problem-statement}

\textbf{SOP corpus.} A plant has a corpus \(D = \{d_1, \dots, d_N\}\) of
\(N = 1{,}012\) SOPs. Each SOP \(d\) has a title, a triggering
condition, and an ordered list of steps \((\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_K)\).
Each step names a skill from a fixed set \(A = \{\)\texttt{walk\_to},
\texttt{pick}, \texttt{place}, \texttt{press\_button},
\texttt{read\_sensor}, \texttt{wait}, \texttt{notify}\(\}\) and an
entity, for example \texttt{press\_button\ estop\_button}.

\textbf{Tickets.} An operator writes a ticket \(q\), free text such as
``line 2 dispenser coasted to a stop, thermal indicator in the red.''
Each ticket has one correct SOP \(d^*(q)\).

\textbf{Retriever.} An encoder \(E_\theta\) maps text to a unit vector
in \(\mathbb{R}^{384}\), and the score of SOP \(d\) for ticket \(q\) is
\(s_\theta(q, d) = E_\theta(q)^\top E_\theta(d)\). The robot acts on
\(\hat d(q) = \arg\max_{d \in D} s_\theta(q, d)\).

\textbf{Execution.} A plan \(\pi(\hat d)\) is the sequence of skills in
\(\hat d\). The robot, with simulator state \(x_t\), executes skill
\(k\) with a controller or a learned policy. Each skill has a
precondition \(\mathrm{pre}_k(x_t) \in \{0, 1\}\) checked before it
starts and a postcondition \(\mathrm{post}_k(x_t) \in \{0, 1\}\) checked
after it ends, both computed from simulator state (for example, ``button
displaced past threshold and held,'' ``box above the table for a
sustained window,'' ``base upright''). The verdict is \emph{resolved} if
every postcondition holds and \emph{partially resolved} or \emph{failed}
otherwise.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Problem 1 (retrieval).} Given training pairs
\(\{(q_j, d^*(q_j))\}\), find \(\theta\) that maximizes
\(\Pr[\hat d(q) = d^*(q)]\) on held-out tickets, measured as Recall@1.
We also report Recall@5 and mean reciprocal rank (MRR).

\textbf{Problem 2 (verified execution).} Given \(\hat d\), execute
\(\pi(\hat d)\) on the humanoid so that each \(\mathrm{post}_k\) holds,
and report a verdict computed only from the \(\mathrm{post}_k\) values,
never from a policy's own success signal.
\end{quote}

\textbf{Difficulty.} Tickets describe symptoms rather than procedures,
and each machine family has about 22 SOPs that differ in small ways
(pressure below setpoint, sudden collapse, above ceiling). The skills
run in sequence on one body in one simulation, so errors propagate:
arriving a few centimeters off makes the next reach harder.

\section{Approach}\label{approach}

\subsection{Retriever}\label{retriever}

We fine-tune \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} {[}5{]} (22.7M parameters, 6
layers) on the 1,859 training pairs. Each SOP is represented as its
title, condition, and steps in one string. Training uses the
multiple-negatives ranking loss: for a batch of \(B\) pairs, each ticket
should score its own SOP above the other \(B - 1\) SOPs in the batch,

\[\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\frac{1}{B} \sum_{j=1}^{B} \log \frac{\exp(\lambda\, s_\theta(q_j, d_j))}{\sum_{l=1}^{B} \exp(\lambda\, s_\theta(q_j, d_l))},\]

with \(B = 32\) and \(\lambda = 20\), for 3 epochs (about 2 minutes on a
laptop). Hyperparameters are in the appendix.

\textbf{Baselines.} All are evaluated on the same index and test set.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{TF-IDF}: unigram and bigram TF-IDF with cosine similarity.
\item
  \textbf{BM25} {[}7{]}: Okapi BM25 over lowercase word tokens.
\item
  \textbf{MiniLM-0}: the same encoder without fine-tuning. This isolates
  what training adds.
\item
  \textbf{bge-small-0}: \texttt{bge-small-en-v1.5}, a stronger
  off-the-shelf encoder of similar size, without fine-tuning.
\item
  \textbf{MiniLM-FT + CE}: our model with its top 10 reranked by the
  off-the-shelf cross-encoder \texttt{ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Planner}\label{planner}

The SOPs are written at skill granularity, so the planner is a
deterministic mapping: one skill per written step, in order, with a
\texttt{walk\_to} inserted when the required location changes. Step
arguments are mapped onto the simulator's objects (four colored buttons
and a fixed set of tools). There is no learning in this step.

\subsection{Skills}\label{skills}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Walk}: the frozen AMO whole-body policy {[}9{]} with a
  closed-loop yaw servo. AMO cannot turn in place and drifts about 18°
  in yaw while walking, so the servo re-steers toward the target from
  the measured base pose.
\item
  \textbf{Pick and place}: damped-least-squares IK to the box, then a
  latch that engages only when both gripper pads measure contact force,
  so a grasp cannot be declared across a gap.
\item
  \textbf{Press}: PPO with a curriculum that moves the arm's start pose
  from touching the button to the true rest pose as the success rate
  rises.
\item
  \textbf{Lever}: behavior cloning from scripted IK demonstrations, one
  DAgger pass, then PPO.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Verifier}\label{verifier}

The verifier wraps every skill with its precondition and postcondition
(Section 2). It is independent of how a skill is implemented, so the
same checks apply to controllers and learned policies. Before the final
system existed, it already separated two grasp checkpoints with similar
training curves: one with a claimed 30\% success was 0\% under
verification (it grasped and never lifted), and one with a claimed 60\%
was 55\%.

\textbf{Generalization.} Every test SOP also has training tickets, so
the test above is closed-set. We add two splits: 20\% of SOPs held out

has its own test set, so only within-split comparisons are meaningful.
The gain is therefore not limited to SOPs seen in training.

\textbf{Ticket sets.} We evaluate the same index on three ticket sets
(Fig. 2): the held-out test tickets (R@1 0.577), the same tickets with
the machine named (0.632), and 92 tickets written by the author from
each sampled SOP's title and condition, two per family, without looking
at model outputs (0.920; the correct SOP is in the top 5 for all 92).
BM25 scores 0.357, 0.398, and 0.837. All methods score above 0.75 on the
title-derived tickets; the fine-tuned model's margin is largest on the
test tickets.

\textbf{Error analysis.} About 70\% of errors on the test tickets select
an SOP for a different machine. Naming the machine raises the share of
top-1 picks on the correct machine from 0.71 to 0.85, and R@1 to 0.63,
so most remaining errors are same-machine SOPs. In one seed, 4 of the 7
errors on the title-derived tickets selected another machine's SOP for
the same symptom although the ticket named the machine. Only 1--3\% of
incorrect picks share the correct SOP's skill sequence, so most errors
would execute a different procedure.

\textbf{Selective retrieval.} The retriever always acts on its top SOP.
We measured the effect of a confidence cutoff on the gap between the top
two scores, with the cutoff chosen on the validation split. Acting only
above the cutoff covers 41\% ± 3\% of test tickets at 86\% ± 3\%
precision, against 58\% when acting on all of them; the remainder would
be referred to a person. This is not part of the system.

\textbf{RL grasping.} RL grasping on the gripper-equipped humanoid
reached 0\% verified success, although the logged lift rate was
30--60\%. Rendered rollouts showed the box being squeezed out of the
pinch and moving upward, which triggered the height check without a
grasp. A stochastic policy's grip force oscillates, and a stable
two-sided pinch is a narrow regime in the contact solver. The IK
controller with a force-gated latch held 11 of 12 lifts. RL grasping did
reach 55\% verified success on an earlier fixed-base rig with a
different hand, so this result is specific to this embodiment and
contact setup.

\textbf{Action range.} Far-start press training failed for a day because
the policy's action range was 0.5 rad per joint around the neutral pose,
while reaching the button from rest needs 0.511 rad at the shoulder and
0.672 rad at the elbow, and the lever needs up to 1.1 rad (Fig. 3).
Saturated actions are not visible in the training curves.

\textbf{Press.} From the rest pose, random exploration never touched the
button, so a contact-based reward gave no signal; a curriculum that
starts the arm on the button and moves it back toward rest was needed to
learn the reach at all. Two bugs cost time along the way: the action
range was smaller than the reach required (Fig. 3), and a collision-mask
error let the gripper pass through the buttons. Each reward change then
produced a new exploit (Table 4). The last one, pumping, came from a
per-step reward for press depth: pressing, releasing, and pressing again
paid more than holding. We did not fix the reward. To run the full
chain, the episode is stopped once the first press has been held for 0.6
s, so in the demos the policy never gets the chance to press again; left
running, it does. We put the remaining time into the end-to-end system,
the verifier, and the retriever. The fix we would make is to pay for one
sustained press as a single event, penalize any release after it, and
evaluate without early stopping.

\textbf{Lever.} The lever needed a latching mechanism so partial
progress persisted, quieter exploration (initial log-std −1.5), and
behavior cloning to reach any reward at all. The best policy completed
the throw in 1 of 2 deterministic episodes, with jerky motion. The cause
is identified: a per-episode reach offset that changes what an action
does is not in the policy's observation, so identical observations call
for different actions. The fix (add it to the observation) is designed
but not trained.

holds.

\textbf{Limitations.} Everything runs in MuJoCo; no hardware transfer
was attempted. The SOPs and tickets come from one generator, and there
is no evaluation on real maintenance tickets. Because SOPs are written
at skill granularity, planning is a lookup, and SOPs written in ordinary
prose would need a learned planner. The retriever cannot decline. Skill
results are small-sample. The press policy still re-presses for reward
when it is not stopped, and the lever succeeds about half the time. The
reward hacks were judged by one reviewer.

\section{Conclusion}\label{conclusion}

Sequent maps an operator's ticket to an approved SOP and executes it on
a simulated humanoid, with a verifier that reports ``partially
resolved'' when a step fails. The fine-tuned retriever reaches R@1 0.577
on the held-out test tickets and 0.92 on title-derived tickets, and its
gain over the untuned model holds on held-out SOPs and equipment
families. On the body, controllers outperformed RL for grasping on this
embodiment. The RL press learned to reach and press but also to re-press
for reward, which we stopped rather than fixed; the lever is partial.
The reward exploits were identified from rendered rollouts, not from
training metrics.

\textbf{Future work.} The most important addition is for the retriever
to ask instead of guess, using the confidence measured above, and to
filter by the machine ID that a real ticket carries. Beyond that:
hard-negative training against same-machine look-alike SOPs; a
comparison against an LLM choosing among the retriever's candidates; a
learned planner for SOPs written in prose; retraining the press so it
stops on its own (reward one sustained press as an event, train without
early stopping); retraining the lever with the missing observation;
moving the body to Isaac Lab for faster contact training; and a test set
of real tickets. Code, data, and results:
\url{https://github.com/jatinsikka/sequent-g1}.

\section*{Appendix}\label{appendix}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Appendix}

\textbf{Retriever.} \texttt{sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2},
mean pooling, 384-dimensional output, maximum sequence length 256.
Multiple-negatives ranking loss with cosine similarity and scale
\(\lambda = 20\). Batch 32, 3 epochs, AdamW with learning rate
\(2 \times 10^{-5}\), linear warmup over the first 10\% of steps. Seeds
0--4 for Table 1 and 0--2 for the other experiments. One epoch gives R@1
0.539 ± 0.013 (3 seeds).

\textbf{Data.} 1,012 SOPs, 46 families × 22 SOPs. 2,338 incidents split
1,859 / 210 / 269 (train / validation / test); the split puts each SOP's
first incident in training, so every test SOP is seen in training.
Held-out-SOP split: 20\% of SOPs sampled with seed 0, all their
incidents used for test. Held-out-family split: five families sampled
with seed 0 (air compressor, filling machine, laser cutter, mechanical
press, press brake). No test ticket has a near-duplicate in training
(highest TF-IDF cosine 0.31). Informed tickets: two SOPs per family
sampled with seed 2026, one ticket each.

\textbf{Confidence cutoff.} Signals: the top-1 score, and the gap
between the top-1 and top-2 scores. For each target precision (0.80,
0.90, 0.95) the cutoff is the lowest one that meets the target on the
validation split; it is then applied to the test split.

\textbf{PPO} {[}13{]}. Stable-Baselines3, MLP policy with two hidden
layers of 128 units, \texttt{n\_steps} 1024 per environment, batch 1024,
10 epochs per update, learning rate \(3 \times 10^{-4}\),
\(\gamma = 0.99\), 200-step episodes. Entropy coefficient 0.02 (press)
and 0.05 (lever, followed by a low-entropy phase). Action range 0.8 rad
(press) and 1.2 rad (lever) around the neutral pose; arm command
low-pass filter α = 0.12. Lever: initial log-std −1.5, 33-dimensional
observation. Training used 24--56 parallel environments on 32- and
64-core Azure VMs. With default threading, parallel workers each spawned
a full set of torch threads and throughput fell to 18 steps/s; setting
\texttt{OMP\_NUM\_THREADS=1} per worker restored about 2,600 steps/s.

\textbf{Simulator.} MuJoCo 3.2.3 {[}15{]}, 29-DoF Unitree G1 with a
Robotiq 2F-85 gripper on the right wrist, AMO at 50 Hz.

\textbf{Reproducing.} \texttt{brain/benchmark/REPRO\_REPORT.md} lists
every retrieval number with the script and CSV that produced it. RL
checkpoints are not released.
